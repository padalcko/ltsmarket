The future of laser hair removal: technology, effectiveness and safety. Part 1.

The professional laser hair removal equipment market continues to evolve. While practitioners previously chose primarily between alexandrite, diode and Nd:YAG lasers, platforms incorporating multiple wavelengths are becoming increasingly common.

The most widely used combination includes 755, 808 or 810, and 1064 nm. Some manufacturers extend this range by introducing additional wavelengths, such as 940 nm.

Technological development has also brought numerous marketing claims. Devices are promoted as being capable of removing every hair colour, treating every skin phototype and targeting follicles at different depths.

What actually makes multi-wavelength technology different? Does a greater number of wavelengths necessarily improve effectiveness? Which technical characteristics should professionals consider when purchasing equipment?

## The physics of laser hair removal

In 1983, Rox Anderson and John Parrish introduced the principle of selective photothermolysis, which became the foundation of many modern laser applications in dermatology and aesthetics.

This principle involves the selective absorption of light energy by specific biological structures and its subsequent conversion into heat.

Substances that absorb light are known as chromophores. The principal chromophores in human skin include melanin, haemoglobin and water.

During laser hair removal, the primary optical target is melanin within the hair and pigmented structures of the hair follicle.

Absorbed energy is converted into heat. This heat spreads to adjacent follicular structures, allowing thermal damage to cells involved in hair growth.

The objective is to achieve sufficient thermal effects while minimising damage to surrounding tissues.

The effectiveness of this process depends not only on wavelength but also on fluence, pulse duration, spot size and epidermal cooling.

## Why does wavelength matter?

Different wavelengths interact with biological tissues in different ways.

Some are absorbed more strongly by melanin, while others generally have more favourable conditions for penetration into deeper layers of the skin.

However, melanin is present not only in hair but also in the epidermis.

Greater epidermal pigmentation can result in more energy being absorbed by the skin before the radiation reaches the hair follicle.

Consequently, parameters suitable for light skin and dark hair may not be appropriate for darker or recently tanned skin.

### 755 nm: relatively high melanin absorption

The 755 nm wavelength is traditionally associated with alexandrite lasers.

It exhibits relatively high melanin absorption compared with longer wavelengths commonly used for hair removal.

This can be advantageous when treating pigmented hair, including finer hair that still contains sufficient melanin.

However, stronger melanin absorption also increases the importance of epidermal pigmentation. Appropriate client assessment and parameter selection according to the device instructions are essential.

The 755 nm wavelength does not enable effective removal of hair that is completely devoid of pigment.

### 808–810 nm: a technological compromise

Diode lasers operating around 808–810 nm have been among the most widely used professional hair removal technologies for many years.

This spectral range provides a combination of relatively good melanin absorption and tissue penetration.

Semiconductor technology enables compact laser modules, high-power diode assemblies and various operating modes.

Modern diode platforms can offer high repetition rates, adjustable pulse durations, large treatment spots and contact cooling.

The engineering flexibility of diode technology has enabled the development of multi-wavelength laser handpieces.

### 940 nm: an additional spectral range

The 940 nm wavelength is incorporated into certain modern diode laser systems.

Melanin absorption in this range is lower than at shorter wavelengths used for hair removal. The relative contribution of other tissue components to optical absorption also changes.

Research involving combinations of 810, 940 and 1060 nm suggests potential applications in treating fine, less pigmented hair in individuals with darker skin phototypes.

However, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that adding 940 nm automatically improves the effectiveness of every device.

The implementation of this technology within a particular platform is what matters.

### 1064 nm: favourable conditions for deeper penetration

The 1064 nm wavelength is traditionally associated with neodymium-doped Nd:YAG lasers.

Compared with 755 and 808 nm, it exhibits lower melanin absorption and generally more favourable conditions for penetration into deeper skin layers.

Reduced absorption by epidermal melanin is one reason why long-pulsed Nd:YAG lasers are widely used for hair removal in darker skin phototypes.

Nevertheless, 1064 nm is not universally superior.

Lower melanin absorption may require different pulse characteristics to achieve the necessary thermal effect.

## Does each wavelength reach a fixed depth?

Marketing illustrations frequently depict three wavelengths reaching three distinct levels of hair follicles.

This is a substantial simplification.

Laser radiation does not stop at a specific depth. Its intensity gradually decreases as a result of absorption and scattering within tissues.

Energy distribution is influenced by skin pigmentation, tissue optical properties, water content, blood supply, wavelength and treatment spot size.

Hair follicles are not arranged at three fixed depths either. Their depth varies according to anatomical location, hair characteristics and growth phase.

It is therefore more accurate to discuss relative differences in penetration rather than assigning each wavelength to a fixed layer of the skin.

Multi-wavelength technology also does not eliminate the need for multiple treatment sessions associated with the hair growth cycle.

## What is a multi-wavelength laser?

The fundamental concept of multi-wavelength technology is to combine radiation with different spectral characteristics.

Depending on the device architecture, individual wavelengths may be emitted simultaneously, sequentially or independently.

Several fundamentally different engineering approaches are available.

### Multi-wavelength diode handpieces

These devices incorporate semiconductor laser diodes designed to generate different wavelengths.

Individual diode groups are combined into a shared laser module. Radiation is then transmitted through an optical system to the working surface of the handpiece.

The most common combination remains 755, 808–810 and 1064 nm.

An important engineering advantage is the ability to incorporate multiple wavelengths within a relatively compact structure.

### Platforms with independent laser sources

Another approach involves devices containing separate laser sources.

For example, a platform may incorporate a genuine 755 nm alexandrite laser and a 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser.

Each source has its own active medium and optical characteristics.

Depending on the architecture, practitioners may use the sources separately or select combined operating modes.

### Hybrid platforms

Certain platforms enable radiation from independent laser sources to be combined while controlling the energy proportions of individual wavelengths.

These solutions provide additional opportunities for personalisation but require appropriate optical engineering, electronics and control systems.

## A triple-wavelength diode laser is not three different laser types

Diode laser specifications frequently contain terms such as Alexandrite 755 nm, Diode 808 nm and Nd:YAG 1064 nm.

However, a laser type is defined not only by its wavelength but also by the active medium in which radiation is generated.

An alexandrite laser uses an alexandrite crystal. An Nd:YAG laser uses a neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet crystal. A diode laser uses a semiconductor structure.

A diode emitting at 755 nm remains a diode laser. A diode emitting at 1064 nm does not become an Nd:YAG laser.

This does not indicate inferior diode technology. It simply describes a different radiation source architecture.

## How is power distributed between wavelengths?

Consider two multi-wavelength handpieces with an identical total optical power of 3000 W.

Their spectral power distribution may nevertheless differ substantially.

Handpiece A:

- 755 nm: 600 W.

- 808 nm: 1800 W.

- 1064 nm: 600 W.

Handpiece B:

- 755 nm: 300 W.

- 808 nm: 1500 W.

- 1064 nm: 1200 W.

Both handpieces generate three wavelengths and have identical total optical power. However, their spectral distributions are different.

This is a hypothetical mathematical example, not the specification of an actual device.

In practice, the proportions depend on the number and characteristics of the diodes, their electrical supply and the efficiency of the optical system.

In handpieces without independent control of individual diode groups, these proportions are primarily determined by the module architecture.

Therefore, the number of wavelengths and nominal handpiece power alone cannot provide a complete assessment of device capabilities.

## What does clinical research show?

Multi-wavelength technologies have been investigated in clinical studies involving different skin phototypes and anatomical treatment areas.

A 2020 study published in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology investigated the combination of 755, 810 and 1064 nm. The authors reported favourable hair reduction outcomes, although the small sample size limits the generalisability of the results. [[2020]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32484620/)

A 2021 study used digital dermoscopy to assess multi-wavelength hair removal outcomes. Mean hair count reduction reached 75.6% at the three-month follow-up. [[2021]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34418269/)

A 2023 study involving the Soprano Titanium platform included 36 participants with skin phototypes I to VI. Following four treatments and three months of observation, mean hair reduction reached 41.5% in the axillary area and 48.1% in the bikini area. [[2023]](https://doi.org/10.1111/jocd.15674)

In 2024, a study investigated the combination of 810, 940 and 1060 nm in 23 women with skin phototypes IV and V. Mean facial hair reduction reached 66%. [[2024]](https://doi.org/10.1111/jocd.16129)

These findings demonstrate the potential of multi-wavelength technologies. Nevertheless, limitations include small sample sizes, different treatment protocols, varying follow-up periods and the absence of direct control groups in some studies.

These results cannot be used to establish a comparative ranking of individual devices.

## Are multi-wavelength lasers always more effective?

A systematic review published in 2022 included five randomised controlled trials involving 223 participants. [[2022]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35634805/)

Reported ranges of long-term hair reduction were:

| Technology | Long-term hair reduction |
| --- | --- |
| Alexandrite laser | 35–84.25% |
| Diode laser | 32.5–69.2% |
| Nd:YAG laser | 30–73.61% |

Differences in methodology, anatomical treatment areas and participant characteristics make direct comparisons difficult.

There is no basis for claiming that every multi-wavelength platform will outperform every single-wavelength laser.

A well-designed single-wavelength device may provide better results than a multi-wavelength platform with insufficient optical power, unstable emission or inadequate optics.

## Multi-wavelength lasers and light-coloured hair

Hair colour is primarily determined by the presence of eumelanin and pheomelanin.

Changing the wavelength influences how radiation is absorbed but cannot replace a missing chromophore.

White, grey and very weakly pigmented hair remain challenging for laser technologies.

Additional wavelengths may expand device capabilities, but they do not eliminate the physical limitations of selective photothermolysis.

Claims of effective removal of every hair colour should therefore be assessed critically.

## Optical power, optics and operational stability

A laser handpiece is a complex optical and thermal system.

In addition to laser diodes, it contains optical components, cooling systems, temperature sensors and control electronics.

Each component influences device performance.

### Actual optical power

Electrical device power, nominal diode module power and actual optical power measured at the handpiece output are different parameters.

They should not be treated as interchangeable values.

### FAC optics

Laser diodes exhibit beam divergence.

Fast Axis Collimation, commonly known as FAC, is an optical technology that reduces divergence along the fast axis of emission.

A properly engineered optical system supports efficient use of laser radiation and beam formation.

### Treatment spot uniformity

Energy distribution across the working surface is important for treatment predictability.

Non-uniform emission may produce areas with different levels of energy exposure.

### Cooling

Diode module temperature influences optical performance and component lifespan.

Cooling the skin contact surface and removing heat from the laser module serve different purposes.

The former supports epidermal protection and client comfort. The latter maintains appropriate operating conditions for the laser source.

Both are important for professional equipment operation.

## How to choose a multi-wavelength laser

Before purchasing equipment, professionals should obtain answers to the following questions:

1. What type of laser source does the device use? 2. Which wavelengths does the handpiece actually generate? 3. How is optical power distributed between wavelengths? 4. Can their proportions be controlled independently? 5. What does the advertised power rating represent? 6. What is the actual optical power at the handpiece output? 7. What are the treatment spot dimensions and characteristics? 8. How does the cooling system operate? 9. Are clinical studies available for the specific model? 10. Are professional servicing and spare parts available? 11. Which documents demonstrate compliance with applicable European requirements? 12. Is appropriate operator training provided?

The answers to these questions offer substantially greater practical value than the number of wavelengths listed in a marketing brochure.

## The future of multi-wavelength technology

Further developments in laser hair removal will probably involve not only additional wavelengths but also more precise control over their application.

Independent spectral control, optical skin analysis, digital dermoscopy and temperature monitoring may support the development of more personalised treatments.

Such technologies require appropriate clinical validation. Automation does not replace professional client assessment or operator knowledge.

Multi-wavelength lasers represent a natural stage in the development of laser hair removal technology.

Combining different wavelengths has a physical rationale and may expand the capabilities of professional equipment.

However, the number of wavelengths is not an independent indicator of device quality.

Equipment selection should consider actual optical power, laser source architecture, pulse characteristics, optics, cooling and access to technical servicing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a triple-wavelength laser always more effective than a single-wavelength laser?

No. Effectiveness also depends on optical power, pulse parameters, optics, cooling and individual skin and hair characteristics.

### Is a 755 nm diode laser an alexandrite laser?

No. The wavelength may be identical, but the active medium and laser source architecture are different.

### Can multi-wavelength lasers remove grey hair?

Hair without melanin remains challenging for laser hair removal. Additional wavelengths do not eliminate this limitation.

### Is handpiece power distributed equally between wavelengths?

Not necessarily. Distribution depends on the laser module architecture and the control of individual diode groups.
