Combined aesthetic technologies: laser hair removal, photorejuvenation and personalised treatment programmes

The future of laser hair removal: technology, effectiveness and safety. Part 3.

For many years, aesthetic clinics specialised in individual treatment categories. Some focused on unwanted hair reduction, while others concentrated on skin appearance, pigmentation or rejuvenation procedures.

Developments in energy-based aesthetic equipment are gradually changing this model. More clinics are using several technologies to address different client needs.

A client receiving laser hair removal may also be interested in improving skin tone, reducing the appearance of visible vessels, improving skin texture or addressing selected scars.

However, this does not mean that all these concerns can be addressed with one laser or during a single appointment.

Understanding the mechanisms, indications and limitations of different technologies is essential when developing combined treatment programmes.

Why do different technologies affect the skin differently?

One of the most important concepts in laser technology is the chromophore.

A chromophore is a component of tissue capable of absorbing light within particular wavelength ranges.

Three chromophores are especially relevant in aesthetic treatments and dermatology:

  • Melanin, which contributes to skin and hair pigmentation.
  • Haemoglobin, which is present in blood.
  • Water, which is a major component of biological tissues.

Each has different optical absorption characteristics.

By selecting appropriate wavelengths and pulse parameters, energy can be directed towards particular tissue structures.

This principle forms the basis of selective photothermolysis, described by Anderson and Parrish in 1983.

However, not every aesthetic technology uses light. Radiofrequency systems rely on the interaction of high-frequency electrical energy with tissue and the resulting thermal effects.

Equipment intended for hair reduction, photorejuvenation, vascular treatments and skin remodelling is therefore not automatically interchangeable.

Laser hair removal: targeting hair follicle structures

In laser hair removal, the primary chromophore is melanin within the hair.

Absorbed optical energy is converted into heat, affecting structures involved in hair growth.

Professional equipment includes:

  • 755 nm alexandrite lasers.
  • Diode lasers, commonly operating around 800–810 nm.
  • 1064 nm Nd:YAG lasers.
  • Multi-wavelength diode platforms.

Treatment effectiveness depends not only on wavelength but also on hair characteristics, skin pigmentation, hair growth phase, pulse parameters and cooling.

The primary objective is long-term hair reduction.

A laser designed for hair removal should not automatically be considered suitable for pigmentation treatments, vascular procedures or skin resurfacing.

Even where a particular wavelength is used for different applications, device architecture, pulse duration, energy delivery and intended use remain important.

IPL: intense pulsed light

Intense pulsed light, or IPL, uses pulses of light covering a broad spectral range.

Unlike a typical laser, an IPL system does not emit one specific wavelength.

The light source is commonly a xenon flashlamp. Optical filters restrict the spectral range reaching the skin.

Depending on device architecture, filters and manufacturer-defined indications, IPL may be used for:

  • Hair reduction.
  • Selected pigmentation concerns.
  • Superficial vascular concerns.
  • Improving the appearance of photodamaged skin.

The versatility of IPL primarily comes from the ability to use different spectral ranges and pulse characteristics.

However, not every IPL device offers equivalent capabilities.

Filter quality, energy stability, pulse characteristics, cooling and technical documentation are all important.

BBL: is it a separate type of laser?

BroadBand Light, or BBL, is the name of a broadband pulsed light technology developed by Sciton.

From a physical perspective, it belongs to the IPL family of technologies.

BBL is not a separate type of laser.

Specific BBL platforms may differ from other IPL devices in handpiece architecture, optical filters, pulse generation, cooling and energy control.

Depending on the platform and its indications, BBL may be used for pigmentation concerns, vascular concerns, signs of photodamage and hair reduction.

The term BBL should not be used as a generic name for every broadband pulsed light device.

When purchasing equipment, practitioners should evaluate the actual specifications and capabilities of the system rather than relying solely on its commercial name.

Photorejuvenation and laser hair removal

Although both procedures can use optical energy, their objectives are different.

Hair removal primarily targets structures within the hair follicle.

IPL or BBL photorejuvenation may aim to improve skin appearance by addressing selected pigmentary and vascular concerns and features associated with photodamage.

Scientific reviews of IPL indicate that the technology can improve the appearance of skin affected by photoageing.

Outcomes depend on the type of concern, equipment, treatment parameters and individual skin characteristics.

Photorejuvenation should not be presented as a universal solution for every pigmentation concern.

Certain conditions, including melasma, require particular caution. Suspicious skin lesions should receive appropriate medical assessment before light-based treatment.

Fractional lasers: controlled skin remodelling

Fractional lasers represent another category of aesthetic equipment.

Their mechanism involves creating microscopic treatment zones separated by areas that are not directly exposed to the laser beam.

This approach forms the basis of fractional photothermolysis, described in scientific literature in 2004.

Fractional technologies can be divided into ablative and non-ablative systems.

Ablative fractional lasers

Examples include:

  • CO2 lasers operating at 10,600 nm.
  • Er:YAG lasers operating at 2,940 nm.

Their primary chromophore is water.

Under appropriate conditions, laser energy removes microscopic areas of tissue and initiates a controlled healing response.

These technologies are used in procedures addressing selected scars, skin texture irregularities and signs of photoageing.

Ablative treatments may involve longer recovery periods and require appropriate client assessment and aftercare.

Non-ablative fractional lasers

Non-ablative technologies create controlled thermal treatment zones without conventional surface ablation.

Examples include certain systems operating around 1,540 or 1,550 nm.

Applications may include improving skin texture, reducing the appearance of selected scars and stimulating tissue remodelling.

Recovery periods may be shorter than those associated with ablative treatments, although this depends on treatment parameters and individual skin responses.

Radiofrequency as a complement to light-based technologies

Radiofrequency, commonly abbreviated as RF, uses high-frequency electrical energy.

Thermal effects arise through the interaction of electrical current with tissue and depend on tissue electrical properties, electrode configuration and device parameters.

Unlike lasers and IPL, RF does not rely directly on selective light absorption by melanin or haemoglobin.

Aesthetic applications include:

  • Monopolar radiofrequency.
  • Bipolar radiofrequency.
  • Multipolar systems.
  • Radiofrequency microneedling.

These technologies differ in energy delivery, treatment depth and safety profile.

RF may form part of programmes intended to improve skin firmness and texture.

However, radiofrequency is not equivalent to laser hair removal or a universal replacement for fractional lasers.

Radiofrequency microneedling disrupts the skin barrier and requires particular attention to client assessment, hygiene and safety.

Can laser hair removal be combined with other treatments?

Different technologies can form part of an individual treatment programme.

However, a combined programme does not necessarily mean performing every procedure during one appointment.

For example, a client may receive a course of laser hair removal and attend a separate IPL appointment addressing a particular pigmentation concern.

Another client may proceed to treatments intended to improve skin texture after completing an appropriate stage of their hair removal programme.

Each procedure should have its own justification and be preceded by appropriate assessment.

When different technologies are used on the same anatomical area, important considerations include:

  • Current skin condition.
  • Response to previous treatments.
  • Cumulative thermal exposure.
  • Skin barrier integrity.
  • Risk of pigmentation changes.
  • Contraindications.
  • Manufacturer recommendations.

There is no universal treatment interval suitable for every possible combination of technologies.

Scheduling should account for treatment type, intensity, recovery and individual skin characteristics.

Using more technologies does not automatically produce better outcomes.

Paradoxical hair growth and the importance of assessment

One less obvious concern associated with light-based treatments is paradoxical hypertrichosis.

This is an unwanted increase in hair growth following laser or IPL treatment.

A meta-analysis published in 2021, covering 22 studies and 9,733 patients, estimated a pooled prevalence of approximately 3%.

The phenomenon was particularly associated with treatment of the face and neck. Reported prevalence outside these areas was substantially lower.

The authors also reported considerable heterogeneity between studies, meaning that this figure should not be interpreted as the precise individual risk associated with every client or device.

These findings highlight the importance of considering hair characteristics, treatment location and appropriate indications for optical energy exposure.

Additional skin areas should not be exposed to light-based treatment simply because the equipment offers that capability.

One multifunctional device or several specialised platforms?

For aesthetic clinic owners, equipment selection also has an economic dimension.

A multifunctional platform may incorporate several handpiece types, enabling different procedures to be performed using one base unit.

Potential advantages include:

  • Reduced space requirements.
  • A shared user interface.
  • Gradual expansion of treatment offerings.
  • Potentially lower initial equipment costs.

However, limitations should also be considered.

Failure of a shared power supply, cooling system or controller may make several treatment functions unavailable simultaneously.

Specialised equipment allows individual treatment stations to operate independently.

This can be particularly useful in clinics where several practitioners perform different procedures at the same time.

Nevertheless, a specialised device is not necessarily more effective than a multifunctional platform. Performance depends on the architecture and specifications of the particular equipment.

Evaluating the economic value of equipment

Equipment purchasing decisions should consider not only the purchase price but also the total cost of ownership.

Relevant expenses include consumables, handpieces, maintenance, repairs, practitioner training and potential downtime.

For multifunctional platforms, it is important to establish whether the advertised technologies are supported by appropriate documentation and whether spare parts are available.

Clinic owners should also identify which treatments correspond to genuine client demand.

Purchasing equipment with numerous functions may not be economically justified if most of those functions remain unused.

Equipment expansion should follow a clinic development strategy rather than the number of features listed in a manufacturer's brochure.

What should you consider before purchasing equipment?

Before making a decision, establish:

  • What is the intended use of each handpiece?
  • Are the advertised functions supported by documentation?
  • What are the actual energy delivery specifications?
  • Can individual modules operate independently?
  • Are spare parts available?
  • What are the consumable costs?
  • Is professional technical support available?
  • What practitioner qualifications and training are required?
  • Does the conformity documentation meet the requirements applicable to the equipment?

For aesthetic equipment using electromagnetic radiation, relevant European regulations should also be considered, including requirements applicable to products covered by Annex XVI of the Medical Device Regulation, where relevant.

Conclusion

Developments in energy-based aesthetics involve more than increasing equipment power or adding new features.

The ability to select and use different technologies according to individual client needs and equipment capabilities is becoming increasingly important.

Laser hair removal, IPL, BBL, fractional lasers and radiofrequency have different mechanisms of action and can complement one another within individually planned treatment programmes.

Combining them requires knowledge, appropriate client assessment and adherence to safety principles.
