Intelligent hair removal lasers: artificial intelligence, melanin analysis and treatment safety

24.11.2026 · Dmytro Zaitsev — CEO, founder and lead technical specialist

The future of laser hair removal: technology, effectiveness and safety. Part 2.

For many years, developments in laser hair removal equipment primarily focused on increasing power, improving cooling and introducing additional wavelengths.

Today, another direction is becoming increasingly important. Equipment can do more than generate laser radiation. It can collect information about the skin, monitor its operating parameters and support practitioners in making decisions.

Melanin measurements, spectrophotometry, digital dermoscopy, temperature sensors and image analysis algorithms create new opportunities for treatment personalisation.

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is also appearing increasingly frequently in equipment descriptions.

However, not every automatic function constitutes artificial intelligence. Understanding the differences between measurement, automation and machine learning is essential when evaluating modern equipment.

Why is skin assessment important?

Laser hair removal is based on selective photothermolysis. Light energy absorbed by melanin within the hair is converted into heat, which affects structures within the hair follicle.

However, melanin is also present in the epidermis.

Some laser energy may be absorbed by the skin before reaching the follicle. The amount absorbed depends on factors including pigmentation, wavelength and pulse characteristics.

Accurate skin assessment is therefore important for both treatment effectiveness and safety.

Practitioners traditionally rely on client interviews, visual assessment and the Fitzpatrick skin phototype classification.

These methods remain necessary but can be supplemented with objective optical measurements.

The Fitzpatrick scale and actual melanin content

The Fitzpatrick classification includes six skin phototypes. It was originally developed to describe skin responses to sunlight, particularly the tendency to burn or tan.

A phototype is not a direct measurement of melanin concentration.

Two individuals with the same phototype may have different levels of pigmentation. Differences may also exist between anatomical areas of the same individual.

Seasonal changes, recent sun exposure and local pigmentation variations are also relevant.

Consequently, phototype alone may not provide all the information required for treatment parameter selection.

Objective pigmentation measurements can provide an additional source of information.

What is skin spectrophotometry?

Spectrophotometry is an optical technique used to analyse how materials absorb or reflect light across different spectral ranges.

In skin assessment, optical measurements can provide information associated with melanin, haemoglobin and other tissue properties.

A simplified measurement process involves the following steps:

  1. A light source illuminates the measurement area.
  2. Some light is absorbed and scattered within the tissue.
  3. A detector records light returning from the skin.
  4. Software analyses the signal.
  5. The result is presented as a pigmentation index or another optical parameter.

Not every colour sensor is a spectrophotometer. Simpler instruments may use a few selected wavelengths, whereas advanced systems analyse broader spectral ranges.

A melanin index should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of the percentage of melanin in the skin.

Results depend on the measurement method, calibration and algorithms used by the instrument.

Is there scientific evidence for melanin measurements?

Quantitative assessment of skin pigmentation has been investigated for many years.

In 2009, Verkruysse and colleagues published a study in the Journal of Biomedical Optics investigating optical measurements of light returning from the skin at 390 nm.

The research involved 23 volunteers and measurements at 749 skin sites.

The authors investigated whether optical measurements could support epidermal pigmentation assessment and estimation of tolerance to laser exposure [1].

In 2016, a publication examined the use of a melanin meter to support parameter selection for intense pulsed light treatments.

Relationships between pigmentation and epidermal tolerance were incorporated into guidance software. The authors also described experience using the device in more than 300 patients [2].

In 2018, Lloyd, Graves and Ross published research on melanin measurements and epidermal tolerance during treatment using long-pulsed 755 nm alexandrite and 1064 nm Nd:YAG lasers.

Their findings supported quantitative pigmentation assessment as an additional tool for informing parameter selection [3].

This does not establish a universal algorithm capable of automatically selecting the settings of every laser.

It does, however, demonstrate a scientific rationale for objective pigmentation assessment.

Analysing skin responses during treatments

Another development involves optical methods for assessing changes in the skin.

In 2019, researchers published a spectroscopic investigation of erythema associated with laser hair removal.

The study involved 19 participants. Skin reflectance spectra were recorded before and after treatment.

The researchers analysed changes associated with the optical characteristics of haemoglobin and the erythema index [4].

Such methods may support the development of tools for more objective assessment of skin responses.

However, this does not demonstrate that modern lasers can automatically identify every thermal injury or prevent all adverse events.

Measuring an individual parameter and comprehensively assessing treatment safety are different tasks.

Temperature sensors and intelligent cooling

Temperature is important both to laser hair removal and to equipment operation.

Modern lasers may incorporate several different temperature sensors:

  • Diode module temperature sensors.
  • Coolant temperature sensors.
  • Handpiece contact surface sensors.
  • Skin surface temperature sensors, where supported by the device architecture.

Each sensor measures a different part of the system.

The temperature of the sapphire contact window is not equivalent to skin temperature. Skin surface temperature also does not directly establish the temperature inside the hair follicle.

These distinctions matter when evaluating systems marketed as intelligent cooling.

Automatic regulation may involve continuously measuring temperature, comparing it with a target value and controlling cooling components accordingly.

This is known as closed-loop or feedback control.

Artificial intelligence is not necessarily required for such a system to operate effectively.

Contact and motion sensors

Certain laser and IPL systems incorporate sensors that assess contact between the handpiece and the skin.

Their purpose may include preventing emission when the handpiece is incorrectly positioned.

Motion sensors are another solution that can support practitioners working in dynamic treatment modes.

Depending on the device architecture, a system may monitor movement, identify deviations or assist with treatment coverage.

These features do not automatically eliminate the risk of excessive skin heating.

Motion sensing does not replace an understanding of energy distribution, treatment overlap and tissue responses.

Examples of contact, pigmentation and motion sensors can also be found in technical documentation for IPL devices [5].

Such functions should not be automatically attributed to every professional diode laser.

Dermoscopy and image analysis

Digital dermoscopy can provide additional information that may not be available through conventional visual skin assessment.

Magnified images allow practitioners to examine visible hair characteristics, distribution, variations in thickness and the skin surface.

Standardised images can also support documentation of changes between appointments.

In advanced systems, images may be transferred to software for analysis of selected features.

Potential applications include automated counting of visible hairs, hair thickness estimation, image comparison and identification of areas requiring further observation.

Accuracy depends on image quality, illumination, magnification and algorithm validation.

The presence of a dermoscope does not itself indicate artificial intelligence.

Dermoscopy does not replace medical dermatological assessment either. Suspicious skin lesions require appropriate medical evaluation.

An example of integrating an additional optical instrument into a hair removal platform is LUMOS, which can be equipped with a dermoscope in selected configurations.

Does every intelligent laser use AI?

From an engineering perspective, several levels of automation should be distinguished.

Level 1. Factory treatment programmes

The operator selects a skin phototype, body area and hair type. The device proposes previously stored settings.

This is a preset-based system and does not necessarily involve artificial intelligence.

Level 2. Rule-based algorithms

Software processes information according to relationships established by the manufacturer.

For example, a pigmentation measurement may be associated with a predefined parameter range.

This can be more advanced than a basic preset but does not require machine learning.

Level 3. Feedback control

The device monitors a selected parameter and responds to changes.

Automatic cooling regulation using temperature measurements is one example.

This is control engineering and can operate without AI.

Level 4. Machine learning

The system uses a model developed from training data.

Such a model may analyse images, classify selected skin or hair characteristics and support decision-making.

Its value depends on data quality, validation and the conditions under which the system was evaluated.

The model does not necessarily learn during each treatment. It may use previously trained parameters.

How might an intelligent operator support system work?

An advanced system may combine information from multiple sources.

During assessment, it may collect information about skin pigmentation, visible hair characteristics and the treatment area.

Software can then relate the measurements to the intended operating conditions of the device and present relevant information or recommendations to the practitioner.

During operation, the system may monitor selected technical parameters, including handpiece temperature or skin contact.

Abnormal conditions may trigger a warning or emission interlock if supported by the equipment architecture.

After treatment, stored information can assist with documentation.

Practitioner control remains essential. Software recommendations should not replace client assessment, knowledge of contraindications or compliance with manufacturer instructions.

Emerging technology and future developments

In 2025, European patent application EP4537779A1 was published, describing a concept for selecting laser handpiece parameters through analysis of skin and hair characteristics.

The document describes the potential use of a hyperspectral camera, additional sensors and neural networks for classifying skin and hair characteristics and generating recommended settings [6].

Hyperspectral imaging captures image information across numerous spectral bands, providing more information than conventional RGB photography.

The patent application illustrates a possible direction for future technology.

However, it does not establish clinical effectiveness or demonstrate that the proposed features are commercially available in every modern device.

When evaluating innovative equipment, it is important to distinguish manufacturer claims, technical documentation, patent applications and clinical research.

Electronic treatment records

Another practical area of development involves storing treatment information.

Records may include appointment dates, anatomical treatment areas, handpiece selection, device settings and practitioner notes.

Such documentation can support the assessment of subsequent appointments and continuity of care in clinics employing several practitioners.

Where photographs or identifiable client information are stored, appropriate data protection and compliance with applicable privacy requirements are necessary.

Maintaining treatment records is not artificial intelligence, but it can be a valuable feature of modern equipment.

What should you consider when buying an AI-enabled laser?

Before purchasing equipment marketed as intelligent, practitioners should ask the manufacturer several questions:

  1. Which parameters does the device actually measure?
  2. Is pigmentation assessment quantitative or based on colour classification?
  3. Where is the temperature sensor located?
  4. Does the device monitor parameters during emission?
  5. Does the software recommend settings or automatically modify them?
  6. Which protective systems operate independently of the AI model?
  7. Are validation results available?
  8. Which skin phototypes and hair characteristics were evaluated?
  9. How is client information stored and protected?
  10. Can the device operate without an internet connection?
  11. Are software updates and technical support available?

If a manufacturer cannot explain which data the system analyses, the AI label alone provides insufficient information about its capabilities.

Will artificial intelligence replace practitioners?

The greatest current potential of intelligent systems lies in supporting practitioners.

Automation may reduce repetitive tasks, improve documentation and provide additional information during skin assessment.

It does not replace client interviews, professional assessment, knowledge of contraindications or the ability to respond to adverse reactions.

Even advanced algorithms require accurate input data and appropriate use of equipment.

The development of artificial intelligence does not eliminate the importance of practitioner experience. Instead, it may provide practitioners with more accurate and useful tools.

Conclusion

The future of laser hair removal will probably involve not only improvements in laser sources but also improvements in the quality of information used during treatments.

Melanin measurements, spectrophotometry, dermoscopy, sensors and automatic control systems may become important elements of this development.

Their value should be assessed through actual functionality, measurement quality and validated capabilities rather than the presence of an AI label.

Frequently asked questions

Does an AI-enabled laser automatically select every treatment parameter?

Not necessarily. Automation depends on the device architecture. Some systems provide only presets or recommendations.

Does skin phototype represent exact melanin content?

No. Phototype primarily describes skin responses to sunlight. Optical measurements can provide additional pigmentation information.

Does intelligent cooling require artificial intelligence?

No. Automatic temperature regulation can use conventional feedback control.

Does a dermoscope indicate the presence of AI?

No. A dermoscope is an optical instrument. AI-based image analysis requires additional software.

Does artificial intelligence eliminate the risk of complications?

No. Intelligent systems may support practitioners but cannot eliminate every risk or replace appropriate client assessment.
